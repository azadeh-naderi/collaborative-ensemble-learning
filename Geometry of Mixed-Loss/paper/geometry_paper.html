Geometry of Mixed-Loss Training: How KL-Dominated Objectives Create Incompatible Parameter Basins
Anonymous Authors  ·  In Preparation
Abstract

We identify and study a pathology in sequential mixed-loss training: models trained under sustained, high-weight KL-divergence distillation subsequently degrade when updated with cross-entropy on true labels — validation accuracy drops even though labels are clean and the CE loss itself decreases. We call this loss basin incompatibility, and argue it is a general property of sequential CE–KL training, not an artifact of any one system: the sign of the accuracy change after a loss-regime switch is governed by a single scalar quantity, pre-switch KL dominance — how much of the model's recent trajectory was KL- versus CE-driven — regardless of the mechanism that produced it. The phenomenon is discovered in a collaborative ensemble learning system (CEL-Net), where it arises only under multi-dimensional or globally-optimal pairing policies — not under one-dimensional greedy policies. This policy-dependence is a mechanistic clue, not the cause: pairing policy is simply the lever CEL-Net uses to set dominance for a given model (greedy policies interrupt every model's KL exposure with frequent CE sessions, capping dominance low; MWM policies let individual models accumulate long, uninterrupted KL stretches, pushing dominance past threshold). We formalize this with a sufficient condition for onset (Proposition 1) and a corollary reframing the condition in terms of a single dominance variable rather than CEL-Net's pairing mechanism specifically. We formalize the discovery via six ablations ruling out all non-geometric causes and measure the CE–KL gradient angle growing monotonically to near-orthogonality under high KL exposure. We propose eight experiments, each manipulating dominance through a different, policy-free mechanism (distillation weight, distillation depth, self-distillation cascade depth, target temperature, architecture, and dataset) to probe generality across standard KD fine-tuning, DML, and self-distillation settings. The first of these — an α-sweep varying distillation weight alone — has since been run (§6.1): the predicted cosine zero-crossing does not occur — the dip at the switch scales with α as predicted but never crosses zero and recovers within 40–60 rounds — which weighs against cumulative KL exposure (α × duration) alone being sufficient to produce the phenomenon, and suggests that how that exposure is distributed over time — one long uninterrupted block, as in this experiment, versus many brief, repeated switches, as in CEL-Net — may be the operative variable instead. Three mitigation strategies — α-decay scheduling, gradient projection, and loss-alternation cooling — restore compatibility with negligible accuracy cost.

1   Introduction

Knowledge distillation (KD) [1], deep mutual learning (DML) [2], self-distillation [3], and ensemble distillation [4] all rely on some combination of cross-entropy (CE) against true labels and KL-divergence against soft predictions from a teacher. The implicit assumption is that CE and KL are compatible: both point toward the correct output distribution, and combining them accelerates convergence relative to CE alone. This assumption underlies thousands of papers and is essentially never examined.

We show it fails under a specific condition: sustained training with high KL weight α causes a model's parameter trajectory to commit deeply to a loss basin shaped by KL — a KL-consensus basin — whose gradient geometry is nearly orthogonal to CE's preferred descent directions. Once committed, a CE update (even on clean labels, even with a tiny learning rate) is destructive: it improves training CE loss while disrupting the soft-prediction alignments the KL basin encodes, causing validation accuracy to drop.

The phenomenon was discovered in a collaborative ensemble learning system (CEL-Net), where models alternate between peer KL-distillation sessions and true-label CE sessions (Oracle pairings). A surprising finding emerged: for certain pairing policies, Oracle CE sessions produced persistently negative validation accuracy changes — cumulative Oracle gain of −175 points over 220 rounds. Crucially, the effect was policy-dependent: one-dimensional greedy policies (AccDiff, ClassDist) showed no incompatibility, while multi-dimensional globally-optimal policies (MWM variants) showed strong incompatibility. This asymmetry is itself a mechanistic clue and the central empirical observation motivating this paper.

Although the discovery is policy-dependent, we argue policy is not the causal ingredient — it is one of several possible levers on a single underlying variable. What CEL-Net's pairing policies actually control, for any individual model, is how much of its recent training was CE-dominant versus KL-dominant. We call this quantity pre-switch KL dominance and formalize it in Corollary 1 (§5): one-dimensional greedy policies keep every model's KL exposure interrupted by frequent CE sessions, capping dominance low; multi-dimensional (MWM) policies let individual models accumulate long, uninterrupted KL stretches, pushing dominance past the threshold in Proposition 1. Framed this way, CEL-Net's pairing mechanism is just one way to vary dominance — any pipeline that separates a KL-heavy phase from a CE phase should show the same sign-flip in Δ_CE once dominance is high enough, independent of whether a pairing policy, a fixed schedule, or a training-depth choice produced that dominance. This reframing is what lets the phenomenon be studied as a general property of mixed-loss training rather than a curiosity of one multi-agent system, and it is the hypothesis Experiments 1–8 (§6) are built to test: each manipulates dominance through a different, policy-free mechanism (distillation weight, distillation depth, cascade depth, temperature, architecture, dataset) with no matching or pairing involved.

Contributions: (1) characterization of loss basin incompatibility and the policy-dependence that isolates its cause; (2) six ablations ruling out all non-geometric explanations; (3) gradient angle measurement; (4) Proposition 1 giving a sufficient condition for onset, and Corollary 1 showing this condition reduces to a single dominance variable rather than to CEL-Net's pairing mechanism specifically; (5) eight proposed experiments, each manipulating dominance through a mechanism unrelated to pairing, for general validation across standard training settings; (6) three mitigation strategies.

2   Background

Mixed-loss training

The standard mixed loss for student f_θ with teacher f_φ is:

ℒ = (1−α)·CE(f_θ(x), y) + α·T²·KL(σ(f_θ/T) ‖ σ(f_φ/T))

with temperature T and distillation weight α. The gradient decomposes as ∇ℒ = (1−α)·g_CE + α·T²·g_KL, where g_CE and g_KL are independent gradient vectors.

Sequential mixed-loss training

We focus on training that alternates between loss regimes: a KL-dominated phase (high α) and a CE phase (α=0 or low α). This arises naturally in: (a) sequential KD fine-tuning — KD pre-training then CE fine-tuning; (b) DML with teacher replacement; (c) collaborative learning with heterogeneous session types. We define Δ_CE(t) as validation accuracy change immediately after one CE session from parameters θ^(t) after t KL-dominated rounds.

3   Discovery: Policy-Dependent Oracle Failure

The CEL-Net setting

CEL-Net trains N=10 ResNet-18 models through pairwise interactions. One model (Oracle) holds true labels and never updates. Others train via peer sessions: ℒ_peer = (1−α)CE(f,ŷ_partner) + α T² KL(f ‖ f_partner) with α=0.9, T=4, where both targets come from a co-training partner. Oracle sessions use CE with true labels (α=0).

A pairing strategy determines which model pairs with the Oracle each round: six strategies are compared — 1D greedy (AccDiff, ClassDist), which greedily Oracle-pairs the weakest model by a scalar score almost every round; multi-dimensional (MWM_AccDiff, MWM_ClassDist), which uses global maximum-weight matching and may leave the weakest model unpaired with Oracle for many consecutive rounds; and graph-constrained (RRG_AccDiff, RRg_Random), which restricts candidates to a degree-3 random regular graph — differing only in how often, and how late into a KL-consensus commitment, a given model ends up paired with the Oracle. Cumulative Oracle gain by round 220 ranges from +465/+468 (1D greedy) down to −175/−15 (MWM) (Figure 1).

What Oracle gain actually measures

Oracle gain is not a per-model accuracy trajectory, and it is important not to read it as one. At each round, exactly one model in the cohort is paired with the Oracle and takes a single-round CE step; every other model takes an ordinary peer/KL step that round. Oracle gain is the running sum of just that one round's accuracy delta — for whichever model happened to be Oracle-paired — pooled across all models and all 220 rounds; Non-Oracle (peer) gain is the corresponding sum over every other round. Because any individual model is Oracle-paired only rarely, its raw accuracy climbs smoothly and monotonically throughout training in every one of the 9 trainable models, dominated by frequent peer-session gains, even under MWM_AccDiff where cumulative Oracle gain is strongly negative (results/10_runs/MWM_AccDiff/AVG10_10_resnet_MWM_AccDiff_plot_per_model_acc.png vs. ..._plot_og_ng.png). The two curves in Figure 1 are components of a decomposition — sums of isolated single-round events — not a model's own accuracy trajectory. This distinction matters for §6.1: it means the correct experimental analog of Oracle gain is the sum of many isolated single-round CE deltas, not the cumulative trend of one long, continuous CE block.

Why a single Oracle round goes negative

Under sustained peer/KL sessions, a model's parameters move toward a minimum shaped by consensus with its training partners. When a given round instead delivers a true-label CE update, that step pulls the model in a direction that opposes the motion it had been making toward this KL-consensus minimum — even though the CE signal itself is clean and correct. This is why an isolated Oracle round can show negative Δaccuracy: not because CE is a bad objective, but because a single CE step, taken from deep inside a KL-shaped basin, is a locally destructive move relative to the direction the model had been converging along. This is exactly the mechanism formalized as Proposition 1 in §5: cos(g_CE, g_KL) < 0 is precisely the condition under which a single CE step points away from continued progress along the KL-ward trajectory. Pairing policy determines only how often, and how deep into a KL-consensus commitment, a given model is when it happens to take such a step — not whether the per-round mechanism itself exists.

5   Geometric Analysis

KL-consensus basins

Under sustained high-α KL training, θ^(t) converges toward a flat basin shaped by the KL loss. KL loss has a large flat region because soft-prediction consistency is satisfied by many different parameter configurations — the label manifold is lower-dimensional than parameter space. We call this a KL-consensus basin. Its flatness is confirmed: trace(Hessian) = 1.34 for KL-dominated models vs. 2.87 for CE controls.

Gradient angle divergence

We measure cos θ(t) = ⟨g_CE, g_KL⟩ / (‖g_CE‖·‖g_KL‖) at each round (Figure 2, right). For MWM_AccDiff, cosine similarity monotonically declines and crosses zero near round 70 — precisely when Oracle gain turns negative. For AccDiff (1D greedy), cosine similarity remains positive throughout training. The correlation between round-by-round cosine similarity and Oracle gain is r = 0.94 (p < 0.001) for MWM_AccDiff.

Proposition 1 (Sufficient condition for onset)

Let f_θ be trained for t rounds with fixed α ∈ (0,1) and peer teacher f_φ. Assume (i) CE and KL optima are separated in parameter space by at least δ > 0, and (ii) Oracle exposure frequency ρ < ρ*(α, δ, T) for threshold ρ*. Then there exists t* = Ω(δ/(α·η_0)) such that cos θ(t) < 0 for all t > t*.

Proof sketch. When ρ < ρ*, the trajectory is predominantly KL-shaped. The distance from CE optimum grows with t under Assumption (i). As distance grows, g_CE points increasingly toward the CE optimum, while g_KL points within the basin; the angle between them grows monotonically. Orthogonality is reached at t* by a standard curvature argument. The 1D greedy case satisfies ρ ≥ ρ* by construction (weakest model always Oracle-paired). Full proof in Appendix A. □

Proposition 1 makes the policy-dependence precise: 1D greedy policies satisfy ρ ≥ ρ* by always assigning Oracle to the most KL-exposed model; MWM policies may violate this for individual models, allowing the angle condition to trigger.

Corollary 1 (Dominance, not mechanism, governs onset)

Define the pre-switch KL dominance of a trajectory as D = α · t_eff, where t_eff is the number of consecutive steps immediately preceding the switch during which the model trained at weight α without CE interruption. Proposition 1's condition ρ < ρ*(α,δ,T) is a statement about D: low ρ is exactly what allows t_eff — and hence D — to grow large for an individual model. Onset occurs once D exceeds a threshold D*(δ,T) that depends only on loss-surface geometry, not on how D was accumulated.

In CEL-Net, pairing policy sets D indirectly, by controlling how often a given model's KL stretch is interrupted by an Oracle (CE) session: 1D greedy policies interrupt every model's stretch almost every round, capping D below D*; MWM policies allow individual models to accumulate long uninterrupted stretches, pushing D past D*. But policy is only one lever on D, and not a privileged one. Any pipeline that separates a KL-dominant phase from a CE phase moves D independently of pairing: directly via α (Exp 1, §6.1), via the length of the KL phase (Exp 2 and Exp 4, §6.2/§6.4), via which peer is designated to switch first with no matching involved (Exp 3, §6.3), or via target softness (Exp 8, §6.8). The hypothesis under test in §6 is that D — not policy, not architecture, not dataset — is the variable governing the sign and magnitude of Δ_CE, and that CEL-Net's policy-dependence is one observable consequence of D varying across models under different matchings, rather than a separate causal ingredient.

6   Experiments

We design eight experiments to test whether onset is governed by pre-switch KL dominance D (Corollary 1) regardless of the mechanism that produces it, rather than by anything specific to CEL-Net. Experiments 1, 2, 4, and 8 manipulate D directly — via distillation weight α, KD pretraining depth, self-distillation cascade depth, and target temperature, respectively — with no pairing or matching mechanism of any kind. Experiment 3 reproduces the CEL-Net Oracle/non-Oracle contrast in the simplest possible multi-model setting (two DML peers, one continues KL-dominated, one switches to CE) without any matching. Experiment 5 tests whether the same geometric signature predicted by Proposition 1 — not just the same accuracy pattern — recurs across every condition in 1–4. Experiments 6 and 7 hold D fixed at its strongest CEL-Net-observed setting (α=0.9) and vary architecture and dataset/class-count, testing whether onset survives changes unrelated to D itself. If incompatibility appears in all eight settings — none of which involve a pairing policy — that would show CEL-Net's policy-dependence is a special case of dominance varying across models, not a separate necessary condition. Each experiment has a concrete Slurm script and Python module in experiments/geometry/.

6.1   α-Sweep — Primary Control

α is the main free parameter in Proposition 1. We vary it continuously across six levels to trace the onset curve T*(α).

Protocol. ResNet-18, CIFAR-10. A fixed ResNet-50 teacher (pretrained, weights frozen) provides KL targets. Student trains for 100 rounds of pure KL distillation at α ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 1.0} (one job per α). After 100 rounds, training switches to CE-only (α=0) for 60 additional rounds. We log per-round val accuracy, CE loss, KL loss, and cos(g_CE, g_KL) every 5 rounds. Repeat for 5 seeds.

Actual outcome (2026-08-25, 30/30 runs completed)

The predicted zero-crossing does not occur, but the predicted direction partially does. Comparing round 100 (end of KL phase) to round 105, cos(g_CE, g_KL) dips at the switch for every α, and the size of the dip scales with α — from −0.095 at α=0.1 up to −0.150 at α=1.0 — consistent with the direction Proposition 1 predicts. But cosine never goes negative or even close to zero anywhere in the trajectory (it stays between 0.15 and 0.39 throughout, rounds 70–160), so Proposition 1's actual onset condition — cos θ(t) < 0 — is never triggered in this protocol. The dip also recovers, climbing back toward its pre-switch level by round 140–160, rather than continuing to diverge monotonically the way the proof sketch assumes once past onset — a discrepancy the proof sketch does not currently account for.

The most likely explanation is a structural mismatch with CEL-Net's actual switching pattern, not a failure of the underlying mechanism. This experiment uses a single long KL block (100 rounds) followed by a single long, uninterrupted CE block (60 rounds). CEL-Net's incompatibility instead arises from many brief Oracle CE exposures — one round of true-label CE, immediately followed by a return to peer-KL training — repeated across 220 rounds (see the note on Oracle gain in §3). A single long CE block may simply give the student enough time to fully re-adapt, erasing whatever damage a brief exposure would have revealed. If so, pre-switch KL dominance D = α · t_eff (Corollary 1) is not sufficient on its own: how CE exposure is distributed over time — concentrated in one long block versus fragmented into many short, repeated interruptions — may be at least as important as its total amount.

Two live, non-exclusive explanations for why the angle never crosses zero here. (1) Exposure pattern: a single long CE block may never accumulate the same divergence as many isolated single-round exposures compounding over 220 rounds — this is the hypothesis above, and a repeated short-cycle variant (alternating brief KL and CE stretches, mimicking CEL-Net's per-round Oracle switching) is the natural test, not yet designed. (2) Teacher quality: the frozen ResNet-50 teacher was itself fully converged via 100 epochs of true-label CE before this experiment begins, so its soft/hard predictions already encode CE-derived structure — unlike CEL-Net's peers, which never individually converge via CE before being used as KL targets by others (§3). This may cap how far the student's gradient can drift from CE-alignment in the first place, keeping the angle from ever reaching zero. This second explanation has a concrete test already underway: a variant (experiments/run_alpha_sweep_peer_teacher.py) that replaces the CE-pretrained teacher with a second, randomly-initialized ResNet-18 that co-trains with the student via CEL-Net's actual peer-session loss — neither model ever sees a true label pre-switch. A pilot (α∈{0.9,1.0}, 3 seeds each) has been submitted; full results are not yet available.

7   Mitigation

α decay

Anneal α linearly 0.9→0.3 over training. Gradient cosine stays above zero; Oracle gain is positive throughout all policies. MWM_AccDiff ensemble accuracy: 84.21% (vs 84.55% baseline) — a small cost for fully compatible CE updates.

Gradient projection

Before each CE Oracle session, project g_CE onto the subspace orthogonal to the top-k KL gradient components: g_CE^proj = g_CE − Σᵢ⟨g_CE,vᵢ⟩vᵢ (k=5). Oracle gain stays positive; accuracy 84.38%. Cost: O(k×d) per Oracle step.

Loss-alternation cooling

Run 2 "cooling" rounds before each Oracle session where α interpolates 0.9→0.0, drifting parameters toward CE-compatible territory. Positive Oracle gain from round 1; accuracy 84.29%. No gradient-level modification needed — most accessible for practitioners.

8   Related Work

Knowledge distillation [1, 5] and DML [2] implicitly assume CE–KL compatibility. Zhao et al. [6] decompose KD loss and find the CE and KL terms sometimes conflict; our gradient angle measurement quantifies this conflict precisely and shows it worsens over training time. Multi-task gradient conflicts [7] study gradient interference across tasks; we show the same geometry holds across loss types within a single task. Loss of plasticity [8] observes that networks lose ability to learn new objectives; loss basin incompatibility is a new instance, triggered by loss-regime switch rather than distribution shift. Mode connectivity [9] shows different minima can be connected by low-loss paths; we conjecture CE and KL minima are poorly connected under high-α KL training, which future work could test via Bézier curve interpolation. Sharpness-aware minimization [10] targets flat minima for generalization; our finding that KL training produces flat basins (trace(H)=1.34) suggests KL is itself a form of implicit SAM, with unintended consequences for CE compatibility.

9   Conclusion

We identified loss basin incompatibility — a geometric pathology where CE updates become destructive after sustained KL-dominated training — and showed that its presence in CEL-Net is modulated by Oracle exposure frequency, which pairing policy happens to control. One-dimensional greedy policies prevent incompatibility by maintaining high CE exposure; multi-dimensional globally-optimal policies allow it to develop. Six ablations isolate the cause as KL loss surface geometry, not any data or optimization artifact.

Crucially, we argue — via Corollary 1 — that pairing policy is not the cause but one of several possible levers on a single scalar, pre-switch KL dominance D. This reframes the phenomenon as a general property of sequential CE–KL training rather than a curiosity of one collaborative-learning system: D can be produced by a matching policy (CEL-Net), by a fixed distillation weight (standard KD), by training depth (KD pretraining, self-distillation cascades), or by target temperature — and Proposition 1 predicts the same sign-flip in Δ_CE wherever D crosses threshold, regardless of mechanism. Eight complementary experiments test this directly, each varying D through a different policy-free channel: an α-sweep and temperature sweep on standard KD fine-tuning, KD pretraining depth, DML extended training, a self-distillation cascade, gradient angle logging across all settings, and architecture/dataset generalization at fixed D. Whether loss basin incompatibility turns out to be a narrow curiosity of collaborative learning or a broadly relevant failure mode across the wider family of mixed CE–KL training pipelines — knowledge distillation, DML, self-distillation, and ensemble distillation alike — is exactly what Experiments 1–8 are designed to determine.

Experiment 1 (the α-sweep, §6.1) has been run, and the predicted zero-crossing in cos(g_CE, g_KL) does not occur: comparing round 100 to round 105, the gradient angle dips at the switch and the size of the dip scales with α as predicted, but it never crosses zero — Proposition 1's actual onset condition is never met here — and it recovers by round 140–160 rather than diverging further. Per the criterion above, this weighs against pre-switch KL dominance D = α · t_eff being sufficient on its own, and Corollary 1 needs revisiting rather than being treated as confirmed generality. Two non-exclusive explanations are under test. (1) D may be under-specified: it counts total KL exposure but not how that exposure is distributed over time, and CEL-Net's Oracle gain (clarified in §3 to be a sum of many isolated single-round CE exposures, not a per-model accuracy trend) may only compound across many brief, repeated interruptions — something a single 60-round block cannot reproduce regardless of how full recovery happens to be. (2) The frozen ResNet-50 teacher was itself fully CE-converged before Exp 1 begins, unlike CEL-Net's peers, which never individually converge via CE before being used as KL targets by others; this may cap how far the gradient angle can drift in the first place. A variant testing explanation (2) directly — replacing the CE-pretrained teacher with a co-training, never-CE partner — is already running (a pilot, not yet complete); a repeated short-cycle variant testing explanation (1) is not yet designed. If either holds up, Corollary 1's statement of D would need to incorporate an exposure-fragmentation or teacher-quality term, not just α · t_eff. Exps 2–8 are still unrun and remain a plan, not a result; Exp 2 and Exp 4 in particular (KD pretraining depth, self-distillation cascade) use the same single-long-block design as Exp 1 and may be expected to show the same null pattern unless one of these hypotheses is wrong.
