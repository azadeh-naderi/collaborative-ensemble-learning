Oracle Gain, Peer Gain, and the Geometry of Mixed-Loss Ensemble Training: A Study of Collaborative Ensemble Learning
Anonymous Submission  ·  Under Review
Abstract

We study Collaborative Ensemble Learning (CEL-Net), a framework where N neural networks train through pairwise interactions. Unlike Deep Mutual Learning, CEL-Net restricts true-label access to a single Oracle; all peer sessions use only pseudo-labels and soft predictions from a partner that is itself still learning. We identify three interlocking findings. First, under multi-dimensional pairing policies (MWM variants, graph-constrained), Oracle CE updates become counterproductive after sustained KL-dominated peer training — validation accuracy drops after pairing with the Oracle. This does not occur under one-dimensional greedy policies (AccDiff, ClassDist), where Oracle gain compounds monotonically throughout training. Ablations show the effect depends on the KL term in peer sessions and on Oracle exposure frequency — consistent with a geometric account involving CE–KL basin incompatibility — but the precise mechanism requires further experiments to confirm, and whether high α is a necessary condition is untested. Second, oracle gain and peer gain compete for the same per-model update budget; pairing strategy determines the allocation, not the total. Third, pairwise disagreement is a poor proxy for ensemble quality: the highest-disagreement policies produce the worst ensemble accuracies, while the best policy (MWM_AccDiff, 84.55% ± 0.36) carries only moderate disagreement. Notably, a single student distilled from the trained ensemble matches ensemble accuracy to within 0.14 points (84.69% distilled vs. 84.55% ensemble), confirming that collaborative blind distillation produces ensemble-level knowledge transferable to a single model at no inference overhead.

1   Introduction

Ensemble methods achieve accuracy gains by aggregating diverse, individually competent classifiers. The standard prescription — train models independently, combine predictions — is reliable but expensive: cost scales linearly with ensemble size [1]. Collaborative training methods attempt to recover ensemble benefits at reduced cost by enabling models to teach one another through knowledge distillation (KD) [2, 3].

Deep Mutual Learning (DML) [2] showed that peers minimizing cross-entropy against true labels and KL-divergence against partners' soft outputs can match independently trained ensembles while converging faster. A key assumption in DML is that every model retains full, continuous access to ground-truth labels. CEL-Net removes this assumption: a single Oracle holds true labels and never updates; all other models train exclusively through peer interactions where both the hard target (argmax of partner logits) and the soft target (partner's full output distribution) come from a model that is itself still learning — the "blind teaching the blind" regime DML was designed to avoid.

We ask: how does restricting label access shape what models learn, how they differ, and whether their ensemble gains from combination? We analyze six pairing strategies and three baselines, uncovering three non-obvious findings for the ensemble and KD communities.

2   Framework

Setup

A population of N homogeneous ResNet-18 networks, sharing all hyperparameters and differing only in random seed, trains for T rounds on CIFAR-10. One model f0 (the Oracle) holds true labels and never updates. At each round, a pairing strategy assigns non-overlapping pairs from the remaining N−1 models plus the Oracle.

Oracle sessions. Model j paired with the Oracle minimizes ℒCE(fj(x), y) against true labels.

Peer sessions. The weaker of two non-Oracle models trains as student:

ℒCEL(θj) = (1−α) ℒCE(fj(x), ŷi) + α T² KL(σ(zj/T) ‖ σ(zi/T))

with T=4, α=0.9, and both targets sourced from partner fi — no true label appears.

Pairing strategies

Six strategies evaluated: a 2×2 grid crossing feature type (scalar accuracy difference vs. per-class Euclidean distance) and matching rule (greedy vs. maximum-weight matching via Blossom algorithm), plus two graph-constrained variants (RRG_AccDiff, RRg_Random) restricting candidates to edges of a random degree-3 regular graph.

Diversity theorem

Theorem 1 (Sufficient condition for diversity, statement TBD) Let Pt be any pairing strategy such that no two models are assigned the same peer partner across all rounds t = 1,…,T, and let initialization seeds differ. Then under mild regularity conditions on the loss landscape, the ensemble's pairwise disagreement is bounded away from zero at any finite T.

Finding 2. Disagreement is neither necessary nor sufficient for ensemble quality. RRg_Random has the highest disagreement (0.122) yet the weakest ensemble gain — its individual models (83.05% avg) are strong but redundant, leaving almost no room for ensembling to add. The lesson: disagreement measures how differently models predict, not how productively they complement each other.

The Ambiguity Decomposition identity — Ensemble Error = Mean Individual Error − Diversity Gain — makes the mechanism precise. MWM_AccDiff's low diversity gain (0.012) coexists with its best ensemble accuracy because its individual learners are already highly accurate; less room remains for ensembling to add. The right objective is diversity gain, not raw disagreement.

Distilled student result

A single student distilled from the trained MWM_AccDiff ensemble reaches 84.69%, matching the ensemble (84.55%) to within 0.14 points — ensemble-level accuracy at single-model inference cost. This holds whether or not the Oracle is included as a distillation source (84.56% without), confirming the knowledge is carried by peer-trained models, not Oracle exposure alone.

4   Finding 1: Negative Oracle Gain (Multi-Dim Policies Only)

In early rounds, Oracle sessions are unambiguously beneficial for all policies — models start near random (∼10%) and improve sharply after each true-label update. For one-dimensional greedy policies (AccDiff, ClassDist), this pattern holds throughout all 220 rounds: Oracle gain compounds monotonically to +465–470 while peer gain stays flat near +45. For multi-dimensional policies (MWM_AccDiff, MWM_ClassDist) a qualitative transition occurs around rounds 50–70: oracle gain turns persistently negative. By round 220, MWM_AccDiff accumulates −175 cumulative accuracy points from Oracle pairings while peer gain reaches +210. Graph-constrained policies (RRG_AccDiff, RRg_Random) show intermediate behavior — Oracle gain peaks early then declines, but remains positive longer than MWM variants.

Finding 1. Negative Oracle gain is a property of multi-dimensional pairing policies (MWM, RRG variants), not of the CEL-Net protocol in general. Under 1D greedy policies, Oracle updates are beneficial throughout. Ablations narrow the cause to the KL term in peer sessions and to reduced Oracle exposure frequency under multi-dim policies — consistent with a hypothesis of CE–KL gradient incompatibility, though the precise geometric mechanism is not yet confirmed experimentally.

Ablation evidence

Six controlled ablations on MWM_AccDiff isolate the cause: resetting optimizer state before each Oracle update, reducing Oracle-phase LR, removing momentum, removing the LR scheduler, and replacing pseudo-labels with ground-truth in the peer CE term. All leave negative Oracle gain intact. Only one intervention eliminates it: removing the KL term from peer sessions entirely (keeping CE against pseudo-labels only). A seventh ablation confirms the mediating variable — forcing MWM to allocate Oracle pairings using the 1D AccDiff rule (always Oracle-pair the weakest model) restores positive Oracle gain even with MWM used for all other pairs.

These ablations rule out optimizer artifacts, learning rate effects, and label quality as causes. The effect depends on (a) the KL term in peer sessions, and (b) the pairing policy's Oracle exposure frequency. Whether the effect requires high α, and how the gradient geometry evolves over training, are open questions addressed by the companion geometry experiments.

Working mechanistic hypothesis

We conjecture that CE and KL navigate toward distinct regions in parameter space. Under 1D greedy policies, the weakest model receives Oracle CE exposure frequently, keeping CE and KL gradient directions from diverging. Under MWM policies, global optimization may assign the weakest model many consecutive peer KL sessions — potentially deepening its commitment to a loss basin shaped by KL objectives. A subsequent CE Oracle step may then be geometrically disruptive: improving training CE loss while disturbing soft-prediction structure the KL training built, causing validation accuracy to drop. The policy choice, not the protocol, determines whether this regime is entered. This hypothesis will be tested directly in the companion geometry experiments (α-sweep, gradient angle logging, architecture/dataset generalization).

5   Finding 3: Oracle and Peer Gain Compete

The three pairing families behave very differently during training yet converge to nearly identical final ensemble accuracy (83–84.5%). This convergence reveals a structural constraint.

1D greedy policies (AccDiff, ClassDist) compound Oracle gain (∼465–470 by round 220) while peer gain stays flat (∼45) — Oracle sessions dominate the learning signal. MWM policies maximize peer gain (∼210) while Oracle gain collapses to −175/−15 — peer sessions dominate, and Oracle sessions are individually harmful in late training. Graph-constrained policies lie between, with Oracle gain peaking near rounds 50–70 then declining, while peer gain grows to ∼165 by round 220.

Despite this dramatic difference in Oracle vs. peer gain allocation, final ensemble accuracy spans only 83.0–84.6% across all six policies — a 1.6-point range. The near-convergence of outcomes across radically different trajectories is the structural observation.

Finding 3. Oracle gain and peer gain are not independently maximizable — they compete for the same per-model update budget. Pairing strategy allocates between them, not the total. Under 1D greedy policies, Oracle gain is high but peer gain is suppressed; under MWM, peer gain is high but Oracle gain is actively negative. The approximately equal final accuracy suggests total learning capacity is fixed at the protocol level, with policy controlling only its distribution between update types.

Hybrid pairing attempts

Patience-based Oracle blocking (exclude a model from Oracle pairing once its moving-average Oracle gain turns negative) keeps Oracle gain non-negative throughout but doesn't reach MWM_AccDiff's 84.55%. Adaptive switching (start AccDiff, switch to MWM when Oracle gain persistently turns negative, after ≥80 rounds) reaches 84.13% — within 0.4 points but not exceeding plain MWM_AccDiff. Removing the Oracle entirely from MWM_AccDiff drops accuracy to 76.45%, confirming Oracle is essential for early-phase learning even when its late-training updates are individually counterproductive.

6   Related Work

Deep ensembles [1] achieve diversity through independent training; KD [3] transfers knowledge via soft targets. DML [2] brought mutual peer distillation to ensemble training but retained full label access for all models. CEL-Net's restricted label regime connects to semi-supervised ensembles [7] and co-training [8]. The oracle failure mode relates to known difficulties combining CE and KD [5, 9], but prior work attributes it to teacher quality; our ablations show it depends instead on the KL term and Oracle exposure frequency. The ambiguity decomposition separating diversity gain from disagreement follows [4].

7   Discussion & Conclusion

(F2) Disagreement is a poor proxy for ensemble quality; diversity gain — the degree to which errors are complementary — is the right metric. (F1) Negative Oracle gain is not universal: it is specific to multi-dimensional pairing policies that reduce Oracle exposure frequency enough that subsequent CE updates become counterproductive. One-dimensional greedy policies, by always Oracle-pairing the weakest model, avoid this regime and compound Oracle gain throughout. The geometric mechanism (CE–KL basin incompatibility) is the leading hypothesis, supported by ablations, but has not yet been confirmed by direct gradient measurement or α-sweep experiments. (F3) Oracle gain and peer gain compete for a fixed budget; pairing strategy allocates between them but cannot expand the total — a constraint visible in the near-equal final ensemble accuracy across all six policies despite radically different training trajectories.

Together these findings suggest that pairing strategy design should be aware of gradient-direction compatibility between CE and KL updates, rather than maximizing disagreement. Practical directions include decaying α as models mature (reducing KL dominance), adaptively routing models away from Oracle pairings if sustained KL training has occurred, or using separate parameter subspaces for CE and KL updates. The lossless distillation result suggests collaborative blind distillation creates broadly useful, compressible representations — a positive signal for the broader question of whether peer interactions alone can substitute for direct supervision.
